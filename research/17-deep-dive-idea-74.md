# 17 — Deep dive: idea 74, AI schedule optimization and coverage forecasting for small-to-mid hospitals and nursing homes (8 September 2026)

*This is a decision document, not a new idea chapter. It re-examines [idea 74](15-healthcare-workforce-planning.md#74-ai-schedule-optimization-and-coverage-forecasting-for-small-to-mid-hospitals-and-nursing-homes) against one further research pass focused on the three things a founder cannot afford to get wrong before writing code: whether the data can actually be gotten out of a small facility without a HIPAA business-associate agreement, whether a forecasting model can work with only one facility's own thin history, and what the founder's real liability exposure is if the tool is wrong about ratio compliance. It does not change idea 74's score (20/30) or its entry in [15-healthcare-workforce-planning.md](15-healthcare-workforce-planning.md). Every fact below carries a source; anything not found is labelled so rather than guessed.*

---

## 1. Thesis and what this deep dive resolves

Idea 74 proposes forecasting next week's patient census and acuity by unit at a hospital or nursing home too small to buy symplr Smart Square or UKG/API Healthcare, then generating a nurse schedule in the same pass that satisfies the applicable state ratio law, the facility's self-scheduling fairness rules and each nurse's contracted hours — replacing the spreadsheet-and-gut-feel process most facilities below system-office scale run today. The original chapter scored it 20/30, capped a point below its cluster-mates because symplr already proves the underlying AI works at enterprise scale; the wedge is price and segment, not capability. That framing survives this pass. What it left underspecified are three questions a founder must answer before committing nine weeks to a build.

**(a) Can the data actually be gotten out without a business-associate agreement?** The original write-up already flags that a patient-level feed forces a BAA and that the MVP should "insist on the aggregate feed to avoid that requirement." This pass finds that recommendation is necessary but harder to execute than it sounds: no system in this segment natively emits a pre-aggregated, de-identified census feed. PointClickCare's own published EHI Export "Census" resource is explicitly patient-level — subject reference, identifiers, room assignment, payer data (ehi-export.pointclickcare.com) — and HL7v2 ADT messages exist specifically to track one named patient's movement, not unit-level counts. Aggregation has to be built, and where it is built determines BA status: HHS's own guidance says a vendor that performs "de-identifying and aggregating data sets containing PHI" *as a service to* a covered entity is acting as a business associate for that service, regardless of what it retains afterward (hhs.gov; accountablehq.com). That means the identifier-stripping step must happen inside the facility's own infrastructure — not the vendor's — for the BAA to be genuinely avoidable. Section 2 works through what that implies for the MVP's architecture.

**(b) Is single-facility forecasting a real cold-start problem, or a solvable one?** symplr markets Smart Square's forecast as "96% accurate" up to 120 days out (symplr.com), with no published detail on how many facilities' history trains that model — a reasonable inference, given Smart Square's 2024 acquisition from AMN Healthcare, one of the largest staffing data aggregators in the US, is that some pooling or benchmarking underlies it, but this is inference, not a sourced fact; **not found** is the honest answer on Smart Square's actual training methodology. What the published academic literature shows, reviewed in section 4, is more useful: single-facility models built on as little as one to two years of a single hospital's own data have repeatedly beaten naive baselines in peer-reviewed studies, which argues the cold-start problem is real but tractable with the right choice of method, not a blocker that requires a fleet of pooled facilities from day one.

**(c) What is the founder's actual liability if the tool says "compliant" and is wrong?** This is a genuine, mostly unresolved risk — treated in section 6 as a mix of sourced facts (insurance costs, the FDA's administrative-software carve-out) and explicit founder judgment about product design, since no comparable small-vendor lawsuit or settlement in this space was found to reason from directly.

## 2. Data and integration reality

**Who dominates the segment.** On the nursing-home side, PointClickCare's own marketing claims, repeated consistently by third parties between 2020 and 2022, put its footprint at roughly 60% of US skilled nursing facilities, serving over 9,000 senior living communities and (per SEC filings around 2015) roughly 10,900 facilities and 850,000 resident-days daily at that time — a dominant but self-reported figure, not an independently audited one; some third-party summaries cite figures as high as 70% (intuitionlabs.ai). Outside long-term care, PointClickCare is a minor player (well under 2% of the broader EHR market by the same source) — a useful reminder this is a deep, narrow niche, exactly the shape idea 63 already targets for PBJ filing. On the hospital side, MEDITECH is the dominant vendor for the segment idea 74 actually targets: roughly 13–15% of the overall US acute-care hospital market by bed count (behind Epic and Oracle Health), but the clear leader among hospitals under 200 beds at close to 40% share, and running in roughly half of US community hospitals with 50–500 beds (sprypt.com); MEDITECH also reports 250+ rural sites live on Expanse, with 15 more added in 2025 (ehr.meditech.com). CPSI's Evident holds a smaller but real position — about 7.9% of the overall EHR market (definitivehc.com) — concentrated specifically in rural and community hospitals, with a reported install base above 1,000 community hospitals, critical-access hospitals and post-acute facilities (healthcare.toolsinfo.com). Epic is the outlier to plan around, not for: a small or critical-access hospital typically cannot afford Epic on its own and instead joins a larger health system's instance through Epic Community Connect, an arrangement Becker's Hospital Review put at roughly $10–30 million in cost (hardware, licences, consulting, staff time and two years of optimization) even for a critical-access hospital (beckershospitalreview.com) — this is not a segment idea 74's MVP should plan to integrate with directly; MEDITECH- and CPSI/Evident-run facilities are the realistic near-term integration targets.

**The mechanism: HL7v2 ADT, not FHIR, for now.** Admit-Discharge-Transfer (ADT) messages — HL7v2's A01 (admit), A02 (transfer), A03 (discharge) and A08 (update) events — are, by design, the record of one named patient's movement through the facility (medplum.com; tactionsoft.com), which is exactly why they cannot be consumed raw without triggering BA status per the reasoning in section 1. But they are also close to universal: roughly 95% of US healthcare organizations still rely on HL7v2 for this traffic, and "every EHR vendor supports it" (thescimus.com; nirmitee.io), because ADT already feeds lab, pharmacy and billing systems at nearly every facility regardless of size. FHIR's Encounter resource is the standards-track replacement, but multiple 2026 integration-engineering sources are blunt that a wholesale ADT-to-FHIR migration "is a multi-year process at hospital scale" and "big-bang" cutovers "are not a thing that ships" (nirmitee.io) — a small facility with no in-house integration team is not going to be the first mover on that migration. The realistic MVP mechanism is therefore to read the facility's *existing* HL7v2 ADT stream through an interface engine, and to perform identifier-stripping and unit-level aggregation at that point — inside the facility's network, on infrastructure the facility (the covered entity) controls, emitting only unit, timestamp, event type and acuity bucket outward to the vendor's servers. Mirth Connect is the standard free tool for this: it carried no licence fee through version 4.5.2 (released September 2024, and permanently available under the MPL 2.0 open-source licence, which cannot be revoked retroactively), though NextGen moved 4.6 and later to a commercial-only licence in March 2025 — BridgeLink, a live open-source fork sponsored by Innovar Healthcare, is the maintained free alternative going forward (meditecs.com; community.openintegrator.com).

**PointClickCare specifically already proves the pattern works technically.** PointClickCare Census Import is a live, certified app on the UKG Marketplace that pulls resident census data out of PointClickCare via API into UKG Pro Workforce Management for volume-based scheduling today (marketplace.ukg.com) — direct evidence that census data already flows out of the dominant SNF EHR through an API in production, even though PointClickCare's own native EHI Export census structure is patient-level and would still need aggregation logic in front of it for this idea's purposes (ehi-export.pointclickcare.com).

**The realistic path for the very first pilots.** Given that small SNFs and community hospitals overwhelmingly lack an in-house integration team to stand up even a free interface-engine channel on day one, the MVP should launch with a manual fallback, not treat it as an afterthought: every one of these EHRs already has a native "census by unit" or occupancy administrative report a scheduling manager can run and export to CSV without any IT involvement. That path is not just the low-effort option — it is also the simplest way to guarantee zero patient-level data ever reaches the vendor's servers at all, which is the cleanest possible answer to the BAA question in section 1. Real-time HL7 ingestion, with the aggregation step running on facility-controlled infrastructure, should be built as an upgrade path for pilots that graduate past the manual stage, not a launch requirement.

## 3. Ratio-law rules engine, verified

**California.** AB 394 (1999) directed the Title 22 §70217 ratios that took effect in 2004; the fact pattern the original chapter cites still holds. This pass adds operational detail that matters for a rules engine specifically: ratios apply continuously, not as a shift average — "staffing ratios may not be averaged across any shift or time period" — and nurse administrators or supervisors with non-direct-care responsibilities do not count toward the ratio (hklaw.com). SB 596, effective 31 January 2026, amended Health & Safety Code §1280.3 and set a concrete penalty escalation this pass located precisely for the first time: **$15,000 for a first standard violation, $30,000 for the second and each subsequent one**, rising to **$75,000 / $100,000 / $125,000** for a violation rated "immediate jeopardy," with each day of noncompliance counted as a separate violation (hklaw.com). The long-delayed acute-psychiatric-hospital ratio finally took effect **1 June 2026** — delayed five months from its original 31 January 2026 date after hospitals warned it would force roughly 800 psychiatric beds to close — and the final regulations closed real loopholes: no averaging a nurse's load to 24 patients across a 12-hour shift, and non-direct-care administrators explicitly barred from counting (mondaq.com; hklaw.com).

**Oregon.** HB 2697, signed 11 August 2023, was the first US law to put numeric ratios directly into statute rather than agency regulation (wsna.org). The ratios by unit: 1:1 for ED trauma, active labor and delivery, and the OR; 1:2 for ICU, non-active labor and delivery, and PACU; 1:3 for intermediate care; 1:4 for pediatrics, oncology, cardiac telemetry and non-trauma ED (as a 12-hour average); 1:5 moving to 1:4 for medical-surgical units; 1:6 for postpartum, antepartum and well-baby; 1:8 for mother-baby units (healthlawinsider.com). Two details make Oregon's rules engine meaningfully different from California's: a charge nurse **can** count toward the ratio "during periods that such charge nurse is taking a patient assignment" — the opposite default from California's blanket exclusion of non-direct-care roles — and Type A or Type B rural hospitals may request a two-year variance from the ratios, an exemption mechanism neither California nor Massachusetts has (healthlawinsider.com). On dates, staffing-plan requirements took effect 1 June 2024, hospital staffing committees were required by 31 December 2024, and civil-penalty enforcement (up to $5,000 per violation) began 1 June 2025; the medical-surgical ratio's move from 1:5 to 1:4 is dated 1 July 2026 by this legal analysis, a few weeks later than the "June 2026" date cited from Oregon's own FAQ in the original chapter — a minor discrepancy between two credible secondary sources this pass could not fully resolve, since Oregon's own FAQ PDF could not be parsed by automated fetch in this pass; use it as a signal to re-check the state's own page directly before the rules engine goes live rather than trusting either secondary source blindly.

**Massachusetts.** This is the state where this pass changes the picture most. 958 CMR 8.00 implements M.G.L. c. 111 §231 (Chapter 155 of the Acts of 2014), effective 28 September 2014, and its scope is narrower than California's or Oregon's by design: it covers **ICUs only**, with each staff nurse assigned 1:1 or 1:2 patients depending on stability as assessed by a DPH-certified acuity tool and by the staff nurses themselves, with the nurse manager or their designee's role limited to *resolving a disagreement* about that acuity assessment — not occupying a counted ratio slot the way a charge nurse can in Oregon (masshpc.gov; law.justia.com). Massachusetts voters had the chance to extend numeric ratios to every hospital unit and rejected it decisively: **Question 1, on the November 2018 ballot, lost 70.2% to 29.8%**, after a record $24.7 million was spent opposing it (ballotpedia.org; bostonmagazine.com). That result means Massachusetts is very unlikely to gain a broader numeric-ratio law in the near term — a materially different legislative-risk profile from California's and Oregon's multi-unit mandates, and a reason not to over-invest engineering effort anticipating Massachusetts expansion. A specific penalty figure tied to actual 958 CMR 8.00 enforcement — as opposed to the failed 2018 measure's proposed $25,000-per-violation civil penalty — was **not found** in this pass; treat the current ICU law's real-world penalty exposure as unconfirmed rather than assume the 2018 figure carries over.

**A schema for a state that isn't hard-coded.** The three states above already differ on four independent axes a flat "if state == California" branch cannot capture cleanly: the ratio number itself, whether it varies by patient acuity (Massachusetts) versus is fixed (California, Oregon), which roles count toward it (Oregon's conditional charge-nurse rule versus California's blanket exclusion), and whether an exemption path exists (Oregon's rural variance; none elsewhere). A maintainable engine should model these as data, not code, in five linked tables: **jurisdictions** (state, statute citation, effective date); **ratio_rules** (jurisdiction, unit type, nurse:patient ratio, acuity tier if applicable, whether it is a hard instantaneous cap or a shift average, phase-in/sunset date — capturing Oregon's dated 1:5→1:4 change as a future-dated row rather than a rewrite); **role_counting_rules** (jurisdiction, role, counts-toward-ratio boolean, condition — capturing the Oregon/California charge-nurse difference as one row each); **exemptions** (jurisdiction, facility type, exemption mechanism, duration, approval required); and **penalty_schedule** (jurisdiction, severity tier, first-offense amount, subsequent-offense amount, whether violations stack per day). A fourth or fifth state — or New York's staffing-committee-and-disclosure regime under Public Health Law §2805-t, which idea 16 already flags as a structurally different, non-numeric rule idea 74's chapter never names explicitly — becomes new rows in these tables, not a new code path. A **generic configurable mode** — a jurisdiction row a facility's own compliance officer can populate directly for any state not yet in the maintained matrix — should ship from week one, carrying a visible disclaimer that a self-entered rule is user-supplied and not vendor-verified against statute, so the founder can take a customer in an unlisted state without a code deploy or a false compliance claim.

## 4. Forecasting approach

The honest starting point is that Smart Square's 96%-accuracy claim cannot be reverse-engineered from public sources: symplr's own pages describe the inputs (census trends, seasonal patterns, staffing standards) but not the training-data scope, and no independent benchmark of it was found (symplr.com). What is available instead is a body of peer-reviewed, single-facility forecasting research that speaks directly to the cold-start question — and it argues against needing a multi-facility fleet before an MVP can be useful.

The most directly relevant study is a 2021 Mayo Clinic project that forecast a single tertiary hospital's inpatient census using **19 months of hourly data** (about 15,000 observations) — comparable in scale to what a small hospital's own history could realistically supply. A linear model with ElasticNet regularization beat both a gradient-boosted tree model and two naive baselines: ±3.4% relative error for the linear model versus ±5.7% for a naive time-of-day baseline, with a mean absolute error of 10.1 patients against a mean census of 524 (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8344501). The simpler model won, at a single facility, on a similar amount of data to what this idea's target segment would actually have. A second, earlier single-facility study forecast NICU census at one hospital using roughly one year of admissions data (1,001 admissions across April 2008–March 2009) with a Poisson-autoregressive arrivals model plus a logistic-regression departures model, and found it delivered statistically significant, meaningfully improved accuracy over forecasts that ignore patient-specific information (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680345; bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com, DOI 1471-2288-13-67). Neither of these is a deep-learning system, and neither needed a second facility's data to work.

The counter-evidence matters too, and cuts toward caution about overclaiming rather than toward needing more data. A 2026 audit of seven forecasting methods against 15 years of UK NHS public demand data, across two documented structural breaks, found that modern machine learning "did not establish a reliable advantage over a strict seasonal-naive baseline" at monthly, system-level resolution (sciencedirect.com, S1386505626003199). That is a genuinely hard, well-resourced benchmark failing to beat naive — a signal that naive seasonal forecasting is a legitimately strong baseline, not a strawman, and that added model complexity does not automatically buy accuracy.

Putting these together, the founder recommendation for this MVP — labelled here as judgment informed by the studies above, not a single settled fact — is: **ship the seasonal-naive/day-of-week baseline first**, in the first week of forecasting work, since it is cheap to build and the literature above says it is a hard number to beat. Then layer a simple regression (linear or Poisson, with day-of-week and admission-source features, in the spirit of the Mayo Clinic and NICU studies) on top, and only present that model's output to a customer once it has demonstrably beaten naive on *that customer's own* holdout data — a per-facility bake-off, not a single model shipped to everyone on faith. This keeps the build inside what a solo founder can do in weeks with off-the-shelf statistics libraries (statsmodels, or a simple Poisson GLM), rather than a research project, and it treats the cold-start problem honestly: real, because a small facility's one to three years of daily data cannot support a heavily parameterized model, but not blocking, because the published single-facility results above show a simple model already clears the naive bar on a comparable amount of data. Cross-facility pooling or transfer learning is not realistic for a v1 — there is no fleet yet at the company level, which is its own cold-start problem — and should be treated as a plausible v2 once several pilot customers exist to pool anonymized patterns from, not a launch dependency.

## 5. Schedule-generation algorithm

Generating a nurse schedule that satisfies ratio law, contracted hours and fairness simultaneously is a well-studied instance of the Nurse Rostering Problem (NRP), and most real-world variants are NP-hard (arxiv.org/html/0910.1253). The academic literature is split on method: some papers report that constraint programming alone is not effective on the hardest instances and favour hybrid approaches — a CP model for structure plus a local-search method like Variable Neighborhood Search for final quality (link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-84882-215-3_16; sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305054817300163). That academic caution is real, but it is aimed at finding *provably optimal* rosters on the hardest published benchmark instances — not at what an MVP actually needs, which is a *feasible, ratio-compliant* schedule quickly.

For that narrower goal, Google's own OR-Tools ships a complete, documented, free nurse/employee-scheduling worked example using its CP-SAT solver — boolean assignment variables, `add_exactly_one`/`add_at_most_one` constraints, implementations in Python, C++, Java and C#, solving its small illustrative case (4 nurses, 3 shifts, 3 days) in about 0.002 seconds (developers.google.com/optimization/scheduling/employee_scheduling). Published benchmark work on realistically sized instances shows CP-SAT's solve time is workable but horizon-sensitive: one comparison found CP-SAT solving a scheduling instance in 0.446 seconds versus 109 seconds for a genetic algorithm, while a larger 21-nurse, 21-week instance took CP-SAT 89 minutes of CPU time to reach a provably optimal solution; the standard public benchmark suite (INRC-II) spans 5 to 80 nurses over one- to eight-week horizons (results synthesized from academic sources including researchgate.net/figure/226289625 and related CP-SAT rostering literature).

The recommendation here — again, founder judgment applied to the evidence above, not a single sourced conclusion — is to build the MVP's schedule generator on OR-Tools CP-SAT rather than a bespoke greedy heuristic or genetic algorithm, for three reasons directly tied to this idea's specific risk profile. First, hard constraints — the ratio law and contracted hours — map exactly onto CP-SAT's boolean/linear constraint model, and the solver either returns a certified feasible schedule or reports infeasibility outright; a greedy heuristic can silently produce a ratio violation, which is precisely the liability exposure section 6 addresses, while CP-SAT's infeasibility signal ("no schedule satisfies the ratio law with your current roster — you are short two FTEs on Thursday nights") is itself a valuable, honest product output rather than a failure mode to hide. Second, CP-SAT is free and Apache-licensed with a documented nurse-scheduling starting point, so the founder is not implementing an academic paper's pseudocode from scratch. Third, new constraint types — a new state's ratio rule, a union contract clause, a new fairness rule — are additional constraints inside the same model, not a rewrite of a hand-tuned heuristic's scoring function, which matters directly for the schema in section 3. The practical trade-off to manage, given the solve-time evidence above, is to bound the solver with a wall-clock time limit (60–120 seconds is a reasonable MVP starting point) and accept its best-found feasible schedule rather than insisting on provable optimality for larger units or longer horizons — standard OR-Tools practice, not a compromise unique to this product. Self-scheduling fairness (weekend, night and holiday distribution; granted shift-swap requests) belongs in the model as a soft objective to maximize on top of the hard feasibility layer, mirroring the two-tier structure of OR-Tools' own worked example.

## 6. Liability and disclaimer design

Two sourced facts bound this problem before it turns into pure guesswork. First, the FDA's clinical-decision-support regulatory boundary works in this product's favor: the FDA's January 2026 final CDS guidance excludes from the Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) definition any software whose intended use is administrative — scheduling tools are named as an example — provided it does not process medical images, in-vitro diagnostic signals or signal-acquisition-system data (fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software; citiprogram.org). A ratio-compliance schedule checker working on aggregate census/acuity buckets and staffing rosters plausibly clears that carve-out, meaningfully reducing FDA regulatory exposure. Second, that carve-out has nothing to do with contractual or tort liability: if a facility relies on the tool's "compliant" flag, publishes a schedule, and a state regulator later finds it wasn't compliant, the facility eats the SB 227-style fine directly and may look to the vendor for redress regardless of what the FDA thinks of the software's classification.

On insurance, tech errors-and-omissions coverage for a generic small SaaS company runs a median of roughly **$126/month ($1,516/year)** for a $1 million per-occurrence / $1 million aggregate policy with a $2,500 deductible (techinsurance.com; insureon.com, both independently reporting the same figure). Neither source publishes a specific number for compliance-adjacent healthcare software, but both flag that companies handling more sensitive data or touching clinical workflows should expect higher premiums and tighter underwriting scrutiny (insureon.com; corgi.insure) — treat $126/month as a floor to budget above, not the number to expect a quote at, and get real quotes before the first paying pilot rather than after an incident.

The product-design recommendation that follows is explicitly **founder judgment**, since no named competitor's actual disclaimer language or a documented lawsuit in this specific niche was found to model it on directly — general industry commentary on scheduling software does converge on the pattern that automated compliance checks are treated as an aid requiring human oversight, not a replacement for it (guideflow.com; simplescheduleai.com), which supports the direction below without proving it. The MVP's hard product rule should be **flag, never auto-publish**: the ratio-compliance engine only ever produces a draft schedule with every violation enumerated in plain language — unit, shift, date, the specific rule and statute section triggered — and a schedule cannot move from draft to published state without an explicit, logged, named confirmation from a credentialed user, whether that means zero violations or an acknowledged override of each one. That reframes the vendor's claim from "this schedule is compliant" (a factual assertion a vendor can be sued for getting wrong) to "here is what the software found against the rules matrix as configured; you, the licensed professional, approved publishing it" — a decision-support framing consistent with the actual boundary of the FDA's own CDS carve-out, not just a legal hedge. Pair it with a retained, timestamped audit log of every publish event recording which matrix version was checked and who confirmed it (useful both for the facility's own regulatory defense and the vendor's), terms of service naming the ratio matrix as informational rather than a substitute for professional or legal compliance review, and E&O coverage secured before the first paying customer rather than after a scare.

## 7. Validation plan (2 weeks, low cost)

**Week 1 — talk to scheduling managers and DONs, build nothing.** Recruit 15 scheduling managers or directors of nursing at facilities below system-office scale — community hospitals via state hospital associations, SNFs via the LeadingAge/AHCA affiliate networks idea 63 already uses, and both via nurse-scheduling-manager LinkedIn and Facebook groups, matching the original chapter's own distribution plan — and get 10 on a call. Ask five questions: (1) How do you check ratio compliance today — spreadsheet, paper, a named tool, or gut feel? (2) Where does a violation actually get caught in practice — self-caught before publishing, caught in a state audit, or not caught until an incident? (3) Would you export a de-identified daily census/acuity count by unit — no patient names or identifiers — to a third party, and if not, what specifically blocks you: IT policy, your EHR vendor's own restrictions, or your compliance office? (4) What do you pay today for scheduling software, if anything? (5) If a tool did nothing but flag every published shift that violates your state's ratio law — no forecasting, no auto-generated schedule — would you pay for that alone, and what would you pay? **Kill criterion:** fewer than 6 of 10 describe ratio-compliance checking specifically (not "scheduling is hard" generally) as a real, named pain, or fewer than 3 of 10 are willing in principle to share a de-identified export.

**Week 1, in parallel — a manual ratio-checker mockup, no software.** Build the state ratio-rules matrix from section 3 as a spreadsheet, not code. Walk 3–5 of the willing contacts through checking one real week of their own published schedule against it by hand, in under an hour each. This tests the narrowest possible version of the product — does the compliance checker *alone*, with no forecasting and no auto-generation, already deliver enough value to pay for — before any solver or forecasting model gets built, the same discipline idea 9's concierge-quote validation used for a completely different product.

**Week 2 — the pricing test.** Ask the same contacts to choose between a $99–149/month ratio-checker-only tier and the full $249–499 forecasting-plus-schedule-generation tier from the original chapter's pricing model, and note which they'd actually commit to rather than which sounds more impressive. This result feeds section 9's refined pricing directly.

**Go/no-go bar.** Proceed to the 10-week build in section 8 only if: (a) at least 6 of 10 week-1 interviewees name ratio-compliance checking as a real, specific pain; (b) at least 3 of those are willing in principle to hand over a de-identified aggregate export; and (c) at least 2 of the 3–5 manual-mockup walkthroughs produce an unprompted "yes, I'd pay" for at least the $99–149 tier. Falling short on (a) or (b) means the data-access assumption in section 1 doesn't hold for this segment and the idea should be shelved, not rescoped. Clearing (a) and (b) but falling short on (c) means build the ratio-checker-only product at the lower price point first, and treat the forecasting-and-generation layer as a later expansion, not the MVP.

## 8. MVP spec, week by week (10 weeks)

This extends the original chapter's 9-week estimate by one week. The extra week is not padding: it comes from splitting what a 9-week plan would have compressed into a single "ingestion" week into two real weeks — a CSV fallback week and a separate real-time HL7 week, because section 2 found small facilities cannot self-serve an HL7 subscription without help — and from giving the human-confirm/audit-log workflow in section 6 its own dedicated week rather than folding it into the schedule-generator week, since this research found it is a hard product requirement, not a UI nicety.

- **Week 1 — validation, not code.** Run section 7's interviews and manual-mockup walkthroughs. In parallel, seed the ratio-rules schema (jurisdictions, ratio_rules, role_counting_rules, exemptions, penalty_schedule) with California, Oregon and Massachusetts data from section 3, since the manual mockup needs it anyway.
- **Week 2 — foundation and the CSV fallback.** Auth, accounts, Stripe billing skeleton, and a CSV-upload ingestion pipeline (unit, date, headcount, acuity bucket columns) that pilot customers can use from day one with zero integration work — this ships before any real-time integration because it is both the fastest path to a first paying customer and, per section 2, the cleanest way to guarantee no patient-level data ever reaches the vendor.
- **Week 3 — the ratio-rules engine.** Build the schema from section 3 into working tables and a compliance-checker function that evaluates any schedule (uploaded or generated) against the matrix and returns a structured violation list: unit, shift, date, rule, statute citation. Ship the generic configurable mode alongside CA/OR/MA.
- **Week 4 — real-time HL7v2 ADT ingestion.** Stand up an HL7v2 listener (Mirth Connect 4.5.2 under its permanent MPL-2.0 licence, or the BridgeLink fork) configured to run on facility-controlled infrastructure, extracting only unit, event type and timestamp — never patient identifiers — and aggregating into unit-level hourly counts before anything leaves the facility's network. This is an upgrade path behind the CSV fallback, not a launch blocker.
- **Week 5 — forecasting v1.** Ship the seasonal-naive/day-of-week baseline first. Build the simple linear/Poisson regression layer with day-of-week and admission-source features on top, plus the per-customer backtest harness that only promotes the regression model over naive when it demonstrably wins on that customer's own holdout data.
- **Week 6 — the schedule generator.** OR-Tools CP-SAT model: hard constraints are the ratio matrix (per-unit, per-shift minimums, role-counting rules) plus contracted hours and no double-booking; soft objective maximizes self-scheduling preference satisfaction and minimizes weekend/night/holiday variance across nurses; wall-clock solve cap of 60–120 seconds returning the best feasible schedule found, with an explicit, named infeasibility message when no compliant schedule exists given the current roster.
- **Week 7 — self-scheduling portal and fairness score.** Nurse-facing shift-preference and swap-request UI, a per-nurse fairness score tracking weekend/night/holiday distribution against their own history, and a swap-approval flow that feeds back into a CP-SAT re-solve.
- **Week 8 — the violation-alert and human-confirm publish workflow.** Section 6's hard rule as shipped product: every draft schedule renders its full plain-language violation list before publish; the publish action is disabled until either zero violations remain or a named, logged user explicitly overrides each specific one; every publish event writes an audit-log entry recording the matrix version checked and the confirming user.
- **Week 9 — pilot onboarding.** A facility setup wizard where selecting a state auto-loads its ratio matrix (with the generic mode for unlisted states), CSV-import validation with plain-language error messages for a non-technical scheduling manager, and a concierge-style hands-on onboarding for the first 3–5 pilot facilities so the founder personally walks each through its first published schedule.
- **Week 10 — accuracy pass, insurance, and launch.** Re-run every one of each pilot facility's trailing-90-day published schedules against the matrix to confirm zero missed violations; backtest the forecast against naive on real pilot data and drop the forecasting feature for any facility where it doesn't win; finalize E&O coverage and the disclaimer language from section 6; open paid billing.

**Not doing:** payroll or time-and-attendance, union CBA grievance handling beyond the fairness score, or replacing an incumbent for a facility that already has UKG or Smart Square — unchanged from the original chapter.

## 9. Unit economics, recap and refined

The original chapter's pricing holds as the primary model: $249/month for up to 100 scheduled staff, $499/month up to 300, blended ARPU around $349, needing 14 facilities for $5k MRR and 29 for $10k MRR. Nothing in this pass overturns that math for the full forecasting-plus-generation product.

What this pass adds is a validated lower entry tier if section 7's validation plan finds the compliance-checker-alone product is what actually sells first. A $99–149/month ratio-checker-only tier — no forecasting, no schedule generation, just the flag-before-publish workflow from section 6 running against manually uploaded or CSV-imported schedules — would need roughly 34–50 facilities to reach $5k MRR and 67–101 to reach $10k MRR at the low end of that range, materially more facilities than the $349 blended-ARPU plan. That tier only makes economic sense as a land-and-expand wedge into the $249/$499 tiers once forecasting and auto-generation are built and trusted, not as a standalone destination price — consistent with how idea 77 elsewhere in this same chapter prices a narrower, compliance-only product around $129/month for the same reason.

On costs, this deep dive surfaces one real line the original chapter didn't itemize: tech E&O / professional liability insurance, roughly $126/month as a generic small-SaaS floor (section 6), likely higher once real quotes come in given the clinical-workflow adjacency — immaterial against $349 ARPU with even a handful of customers, but a line to budget from the first pilot rather than add after an incident. The interface-engine and solver costs are effectively zero: Mirth Connect 4.5.2/BridgeLink carries no licence fee, and OR-Tools CP-SAT is Apache-licensed and free. Net effect: the unit economics remain gross-margin-favorable at the original $349 blended ARPU; the meaningful addition from this pass is the insurance line and the validated cheaper entry tier as a fallback path, not a fundamental repricing.

## 10. Risks and kill criteria

1. **Data-access kill criterion.** If fewer than half of the 15 contacted scheduling managers will share even a de-identified aggregate export in principle (section 7's go/no-go bar), the forecasting layer is unbuildable at MVP stage — fall back to the CSV-only ratio-checker product at the lower price point, or stop.
2. **Forecasting-quality kill criterion.** Once 90+ days of a real pilot facility's own data exists, if the best simple model from section 4 cannot beat a plain seasonal-naive baseline on that facility's own holdout data, do not ship forecasting for that facility at all. An unreliable forecast is worse for a DON's trust than no forecast, and the 2026 NHS finding in section 4 shows a well-resourced team can genuinely fail to beat naive — treat that as a live possibility, not a hypothetical.
3. **Liability/insurance-economics kill criterion.** If real E&O quotes for a compliance-adjacent nurse-scheduling product come back materially above the ~$126/month generic floor — several hundred dollars a month plus a large per-claim deductible — recompute margin at the $349 blended ARPU; if it erodes gross margin below the healthy-SaaS bar this research repo uses elsewhere, either raise price, reframe the product away from "compliance" toward "compliance-assist" to lower underwriting risk, or reconsider the segment.
4. **Ratio-matrix drift risk.** California's psychiatric-hospital ratio took until 2026 to finalize after a 27-year statutory gap, and Oregon's own med-surg ratio changes again in 2026 — states will keep amending these laws. If the founder cannot commit a recurring few hours monthly to tracking amendments (state DPH bulletins, LegiScan), a stale ratio in the engine is itself a liability event, not a maintenance backlog item, and should trigger an immediate fix-and-notify response, not a sprint-planning ticket.
5. **Competitive down-market risk.** symplr Smart Square already proves the forecasting-plus-scheduling AI works at 96% claimed accuracy at enterprise scale — the reason the original score capped at 20/30. If Smart Square, UKG or OnShift push a small-facility-priced tier down-market before this MVP reaches meaningful revenue, the defensible remainder is the multi-state ratio-rules-engine-as-a-service and the sub-$500/month price point specifically, not the forecasting technique itself — track competitor pricing pages quarterly as an explicit pivot signal.
